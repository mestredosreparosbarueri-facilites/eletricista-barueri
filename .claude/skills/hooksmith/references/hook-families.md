# Hook Families

Use when a hook set needs more variety than the core frameworks give, or when every option is starting to sound the same.

Pick the family by what the creator can truthfully carry: a belief, a warning, a result, a story, or a flipped assumption.

## The Six Families

### Philosophy Hook

Lead with a belief or worldview.

Formula:

`Most [audience] are solving [surface problem] when the real issue is [deeper problem].`

Use for belief-led content, creator principles, operator lessons, and identity shifts.

### Negative Hook

Open with a blunt warning, mistake, or behavior to stop.

Formula:

`Stop [common action] if [specific condition is still true].`

Use for mistake breakdowns, urgent fixes, and myth correction. The warning targets the behavior, never the viewer.

### Question Hook

Ask a question that names a pain, fear, curiosity gap, or objection.

Formula:

`What if [problem] is happening because of [unexpected cause]?`

Use for self-identification and curiosity-led education. The question must carry a specific claim. Hedged questions are an anti-pattern; see `anti-patterns.md`.

### Achievement Hook

Lead with a true result or transformation.

Formula:

`I got [specific result] without [expected sacrifice] by [mechanism].`

Only when the proof is true. If proof is unavailable, use another family. Never invent the result.

### Experience Hook

Start from a real moment, test, mistake, or conversation.

Formula:

`I tested [specific thing] and realized [unexpected lesson].`

Use for story-led or founder-led content.

### Contrarian Hook

Flip a popular belief the creator can defend.

Formula:

`[Popular advice] breaks when [specific missing condition].`

Aim at beliefs and behaviors, not protected groups or private people.

## Named Patterns

Proven shapes to fill with the creator's niche and proof. Do not paste them blindly.

### Industry Mistake

`The #1 mistake new [audience] make is...`

Works through fear of failure plus niche specificity.

### Novel Curiosity

`My new favorite thing to do is [unexpected behavior].`

Unexpected behavior creates a curiosity gap.

### Pattern Interrupt Reminder

`Oh yeah, one more thing before I forget...`

Sounds like accidental disclosure and creates urgency.

### Personal Accountability

`The biggest mistake I made with [topic] was...`

Authority through vulnerability and a lesson learned the hard way. Only with a real mistake.

### Reassurance And Validation

`If you are worried that [fear], remember this...`

Meets an existing emotional pain point.

### Comparative Upgrade

`You have seen [normal thing], but have you seen [better or weirder thing]?`

Creates dissatisfaction with the baseline.

### Ultimate Guide Authority

`The ultimate guide for [specific audience] on [specific problem].`

Signals density, clarity, and a defined audience. Needs a body that actually delivers density.

### Stakes Hook

`I could not stop thinking about [specific problem], so I wrote down [number] things that explain it.`

Makes information feel discovered under pressure, not assembled as tips.

### Zeigarnik Hook

`Before I tell you [answer], you need to understand why [unexpected setup].`

Delays the answer while giving the viewer a reason to stay.

### Retention Reframe

`Your [assumed problem] is not the problem. [Real problem] is.`

Reframes the problem and opens a second question.

## Variation Rule

When asked for multiple hooks, vary the psychology, not just the wording. A real set mixes families and jobs:

- one self-identification hook
- one stakes hook
- one open-loop hook
- one contrarian hook
- one experience hook
- one demo or visual hook

If two options share the same family and the same job, replace one.
